Rural Americans face distance, cost, and attorney shortages that keep them from the civil justice system. Online dispute resolution is often proposed as the remedy, and critics respond that it offers rural litigants access without justice. This Note argues that the disagreement resolves at the level of design. Evidence from virtual mediation, from Utah's small claims platform, and from Victoria's tribunal pilot shows that well-designed online processes can match in-person processes, while the failures of form-centered platforms confirm the critics' concerns. The Note proposes an ODR system built for rural litigants on five commitments: certified trust, a guided and facilitated process that does not depend on literacy, due process enforced by design and by amended service-of-process rules, physical access points in public libraries staffed by funded navigators, and continuous evaluation against rural outcomes, sustained by recurring state funding. It applies the system to debt collection, where the critics' objections are strongest, and to divorce, including cases involving domestic abuse, where the platform must screen for and guard against digital coercion. It also answers the four counterarguments that survive those design choices.
